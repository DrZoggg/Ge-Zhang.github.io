## Official Abstract

### Objectives

Despite the crucial role of sleep quality in hyperuricemia onset and progression, there is limited evidence on sleep interventions to improve outcomes for hyperuricemic individuals. This study aims to investigate the effects of sleep duration and sleep difficulties on all-cause mortality in this population.

### Materials and methods

We conducted a secondary analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data from 2007 to 2018, including 5,837 participants. We employed weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazard models to evaluate the independent predictive value of sleep duration and trouble for all-cause mortality. Restricted cubic splines and segmented Cox proportional hazard models were used to examine threshold effects.

### Results

During a mean follow-up of 6.5 years, 906 participants experienced all-cause mortality. After adjusting for confounders, both short (< 7 h; HR = 1.25; 95%CI: 1.04, 1.51; p = 0.018) and long (>9 h; HR = 1.50; 95%CI: 1.10, 2.04; p = 0.011) sleep durations were associated with increased all-cause mortality. The threshold analysis identified an optimal sleep duration of 7.23 h, and when sleep duration was below 7.23 h, it was inversely related to mortality (HR: 0.879; 95% CI: 0.788, 0.981; p = 0.022). Conversely, when sleep duration exceeded 7.23 h, it was positively associated with mortality (HR: 1.187; 95% CI: 1.066, 1.320; p = 0.002).

### Conclusion

Sleep duration is U-shapedly associated with all-cause mortality among individuals with hyperuricemia in the United States. However sleep trouble was not associated with all-cause mortality. Maintaining optimal sleep duration helps improve the prognostic survival rates of those with hyperuricemia.

This secondary linked-mortality analysis examines self-reported sleep in a complex-survey population. ## What This Study Adds

- Sleep-duration and mortality association evidence in a population with hyperuricemia.
- A distinction between sleep duration and the self-reported sleep-trouble indicator.
- Explicit interpretation boundaries for the nonlinear sleep-duration estimate.

## Evidence Scope

### Supports

- Survey-weighted associations of self-reported sleep duration with all-cause mortality in the analyzed population.
- A separate sleep-trouble estimate and a dataset-derived nonlinear turning point.

### Does Not Establish

- That 7.23 h is a precise personal target for everyone.
- That extending or shortening sleep has been proven to reduce deaths.
- A clinical prediction model or absence of risk from all sleep disorders.

### Limitations

- Self-reported exposure and a single baseline assessment limit exposure characterization.
- Residual confounding and reverse causation remain possible.
- Complex-survey weighting must be retained; raw counts and weighted percentages are not interchangeable.
- The data-derived turning point is non-causal and not an intervention-validated target.
- Self-reported sleep duration is not a direct circadian-rhythm measurement.

In 5,837 NHANES participants with hyperuricemia, with 906 deaths and mean follow-up of 6.5 years, Model 3 associations were HR 1.25 (95% CI 1.04–1.51) for <7 h and HR 1.50 (95% CI 1.10–2.04) for >9 h versus 7–9 h. ## Q&A

### What design, sample and mortality follow-up were used?

The NHANES 2007–2018 analytic sample included 5,837 participants and 906 deaths, with a mean follow-up of 6.5 years and survey-weighted Cox analysis. Raw participant counts and survey-weighted percentages are different quantities and must not be directly substituted for one another.

Evidence: KF1

### How was short sleep associated with mortality?

In fully adjusted Model 3, short sleep (<7 h versus 7–9 h) was associated with all-cause mortality at HR 1.25 (95% CI 1.04–1.51). This is an observational association, not a randomized intervention that shortens or extends sleep.

Evidence: KF2

### How was long sleep associated with mortality?

In Model 3, long sleep (>9 h versus 7–9 h) was associated with all-cause mortality at HR 1.50 (95% CI 1.10–2.04). The relative hazard is not an absolute-risk estimate or an individual prognosis.

Evidence: KF3

### What did the sleep-trouble analysis show?

Self-reported sleep trouble did not show a statistically significant association with mortality in Model 3: HR 1.04 (95% CI 0.87–1.25), P=0.651. This result for one self-reported indicator does not prove that all sleep disorders have no effect on mortality.

Evidence: KF4

### Should 7.23 hours be interpreted as a prescribed individual target?

Restricted cubic splines showed nonlinearity (P<0.001), with an estimated sleep-duration turning point of 7.23 h. The turning point is a model estimate from this dataset, not an intervention-validated sleep prescription or a precise target for every individual.

Evidence: KF5
