Research Question

How do circadian clock disruption and time-of-day biology interact with immune-cell trafficking, inflammatory signaling and vascular homeostasis across cardiovascular diseases, and what level of evidence supports translating these rhythms into cardiovascular chronotherapy?

Author Evidence Summary

This narrative review synthesizes experimental, mechanistic, epidemiological and clinical literature linking the circadian clock to cardiovascular immune and inflammatory biology. It organizes evidence around the CLOCK-BMAL1 transcriptional network, PER/CRY feedback, REV-ERB/ROR regulation, redox-sensitive signaling, NF-kB and NLRP3 inflammasome activity, cytokine and chemokine rhythms, and time-dependent immune-cell trafficking. These mechanisms are discussed across atherosclerosis, stroke, ischemic heart disease and heart failure. The review further evaluates preclinical chronotherapeutic strategies targeting clock and inflammatory pathways and contrasts them with a more limited and heterogeneous clinical evidence base. It does not report new experimental or clinical data, perform a meta-analysis, establish a universal cardiovascular dosing schedule, or demonstrate clinical efficacy for experimental clock-targeting compounds.

Key Findings

KF1 The review places CLOCK-BMAL1, PER/CRY and REV-ERB/ROR feedback loops at the center of temporal regulation of cardiovascular immune biology.

This is a synthesis of prior molecular and experimental literature rather than a new clock experiment.

Core clock machinery
BMAL1/ARNTL, CLOCK, PER1/2/3, CRY1/2, REV-ERB alpha/beta and ROR alpha/gamma are integrated with redox and epigenetic regulation.

Source locator: Sections 2.1-2.4; Figures 1-2; Tables 1-2.

KF2 Circadian timing is synthesized as a regulator of PRR, NF-kB and NLRP3 inflammatory signaling and of cytokine, chemokine and adhesion-molecule rhythms.

These mechanisms derive from heterogeneous experimental systems and should not be represented as one fully demonstrated causal human pathway.

Inflammatory networks
The review discusses IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-18, TNF-alpha, CCL2, CXCL12, ICAM1, VCAM1 and related inflammatory networks.

Source locator: Sections 3-4; Figure 3; Table 3.

KF3 The review describes circadian variation in immune-cell number, trafficking and function across neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, lymphocytes and dendritic cells.

Timing, phase and mechanisms vary by species, tissue and experimental model.

Trafficking mechanisms
BMAL1, chemokine axes, sympathetic signaling and cell-intrinsic clocks are synthesized as contributors to rhythmic immune trafficking and function.

Source locator: Section 5; Figures 4-5.

KF4 Circadian disruption is reviewed in relation to atherosclerosis, stroke, ischemic heart disease/myocardial infarction and heart failure through overlapping inflammatory, endothelial, metabolic and oxidative-stress mechanisms.

Evidence strength and causality differ materially across disease, mechanism and model.

Disease domains
The synthesis compares atherosclerosis, stroke, ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction and heart failure rather than reporting one new cross-disease experiment.

Source locator: Sections 6.1-6.4; Figures 6-8.

KF5 Preclinical studies provide a mechanistic rationale for time-dependent intervention targeting clock and inflammatory pathways.

This evidence is predominantly preclinical and must not be converted into human treatment-efficacy claims.

Preclinical strategies
The review discusses REV-ERB agonists, NLRP3 inhibitors, NF-kB pathway modulation, CCR2/CCL2 and CXCL12/CXCR4 strategies.

Source locator: Section 7.1; Table 4.

KF6 Clinical chronotherapy evidence is heterogeneous and does not define a universal optimal dosing time for most cardiovascular drugs.

Physiological timing effects and mechanistic rationale are not equivalent to proven reductions in clinical outcomes.

Review Design & Evidence Synthesis

Review profile

What This Review Adds

  • Provides an integrated immunovascular framework linking molecular circadian clocks to inflammatory signaling, leukocyte trafficking and cardiovascular disease biology.
  • Connects molecular mechanisms with disease-specific evidence across atherosclerosis, stroke, ischemic heart disease and heart failure.
  • Separates mechanistically attractive preclinical chronotherapy from the substantially less settled clinical evidence base.
  • Highlights why individualized circadian treatment strategies require stronger clinical validation rather than assuming a universal optimal dosing time.

Evidence Scope

Supports

  • Prior evidence links circadian clock networks with immune and vascular processes, inflammatory and redox pathways.
  • Immune-cell number, trafficking and functional state can vary with circadian timing across experimental contexts.
  • Experimental circadian disruption can alter cardiovascular inflammatory phenotypes, while cardiovascular physiology and events exhibit time-of-day patterns.
  • Preclinical evidence provides a mechanistic rationale for chronotherapy; some cardiovascular drugs show dosing-time-dependent physiological effects.
  • Clinical chronotherapy remains an active and unresolved translational field.

Does Not Establish

  • A new patient cohort, new experimental data or a newly analyzed dataset.
  • A systematic review, meta-analysis or pooled quantitative effect size.
  • One universally causal circadian pathway across all cardiovascular diseases.
  • Human cardiovascular treatment efficacy of REV-ERB agonists or time-targeted NLRP3 or NF-kB inhibition.
  • Universal superiority of bedtime antihypertensive dosing or a dosing schedule suitable for every patient.
  • Reduced myocardial infarction or stroke outcomes from bedtime aspirin.
  • Established optimal dosing for SGLT2 inhibitors, colchicine, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists or ARNI.
  • A guideline-level recommendation to change cardiovascular medication timing.

Limitations

  • This is a narrative, non-systematic evidence synthesis with no new dataset or pooled effect estimate.
  • Species, tissues, disease models and evidence levels are heterogeneous; causal animal or cell findings cannot automatically be generalized to human cardiovascular disease.
  • Constitutive and inducible clock-gene knockout models may yield different findings.
  • Treatment-timing evidence is uneven across cardiovascular drug classes and many proposed chronotherapy windows remain theoretical or preclinical.
  • Clinical antihypertensive timing evidence is conflicting, including Hygia-versus-BedMed differences.
  • No universal dosing recommendation is established; high-quality circadian-aware randomized clinical trials are needed.

Methodological & Translation Boundaries

  1. This is a narrative evidence synthesis, not a systematic review or quantitative meta-analysis.
  2. The 392 references are bibliography entries across heterogeneous evidence types, not a synthetic study count or sample size.
  3. Mechanistic rhythmicity, animal chronopharmacology, human surrogate physiology and hard cardiovascular outcomes are different evidence levels.
  4. Mouse Zeitgeber-time findings must not be converted directly into a human clock-time dosing prescription.
  5. Changes in platelet activity, inflammatory biomarkers, blood pressure or other surrogate measures do not by themselves prove reductions in myocardial infarction, stroke or mortality.
  6. Conflicting antihypertensive timing evidence remains conflicting; it does not support a universal bedtime-dosing recommendation.

Q&A

What type of article is this?

It is a narrative Review Article synthesizing prior evidence. It reports no new experimental or clinical dataset, systematic study-selection workflow or pooled meta-analysis.

Evidence: KF1, KF7

Which circadian clock components are central to cardiovascular immune regulation?

The review centers on CLOCK-BMAL1, PER/CRY and REV-ERB/ROR feedback networks, including redox and epigenetic regulation; these are synthesized from prior work rather than tested as one new experiment.

Evidence: KF1

How does circadian disruption interact with NF-kB and NLRP3?

The review links clock and redox regulation with pattern-recognition receptor, NF-kB and NLRP3 inflammatory signaling, cytokines and chemokines. The underlying models are heterogeneous and do not establish one complete causal pathway in humans.

Evidence: KF1, KF2

Which immune cells show circadian trafficking or functional rhythms?

Prior evidence discussed for neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, lymphocytes and dendritic cells indicates rhythmic numbers, trafficking or function, with phase and mechanism varying across species and tissues.

Evidence: KF3

Which cardiovascular diseases are discussed?

The review discusses atherosclerosis, stroke, ischemic heart disease and myocardial infarction, and heart failure, with hypertension considered in blood-pressure chronotherapy. Evidence strength differs across disease contexts.

Evidence: KF4, KF6

Does the review prove that REV-ERB agonists treat cardiovascular disease in humans?

No. REV-ERB agonist chronotherapy is discussed as mechanistic and preclinical rationale, not demonstrated human cardiovascular treatment efficacy.

Evidence: KF5, KF8

Should all antihypertensive medications be taken at bedtime?

No. Clinical evidence conflicts: Hygia and related studies reported bedtime-dosing benefits, while BedMed did not reproduce a cardiovascular-event benefit. The review does not support a universal bedtime recommendation.

Evidence: KF6, KF8

Does bedtime aspirin have proven myocardial infarction or stroke outcome benefit?

No large randomized trial cited by this review directly establishes reduced overall myocardial infarction or stroke incidence from bedtime aspirin dosing; physiological timing effects do not prove event reduction.

Evidence: KF6, KF8

Are SGLT2-inhibitor, colchicine, MRA or ARNI dosing times clinically established?

No. The review discusses these drug classes, but does not establish clinically validated optimal timing or universal cardiovascular outcome benefits for them.

Evidence: KF6, KF8

Is this a systematic review or meta-analysis?

No. The article reports no explicit systematic-search strategy, PRISMA study-selection workflow or quantitative meta-analysis; its 392 references are bibliography entries, not a systematic count of independent studies.

Evidence: KF7

What gaps remain before cardiovascular chronotherapy can become precision medicine?

Constitutive-versus-inducible model differences, immune-subpopulation and spatiotemporal heterogeneity, individualized rhythm variation and high-quality circadian-aware clinical trials remain important gaps.

Evidence: KF7
