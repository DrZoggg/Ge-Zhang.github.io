Official Abstract

Background

Cardiovascular‐liver‐metabolic multimorbidity (CLMM), defined as the co‐occurrence of at least two of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease, is an increasing health burden. We evaluated the cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein, and glucose (CHG) index and its obesity‐ and inflammation‐derived variants for predicting incident CLMM.

Methods

This prospective cohort included 6,534 adults aged ≥45 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. CHG, eight obesity‐derived indices, and CHG‐CRP were assessed, with the triglyceride‐glucose index and TG/HDL‐C ratio as comparators. Associations with incident CLMM were evaluated using logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, and threshold analysis. Predictive performance was compared using receiver operating characteristic analysis, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and the net reclassification improvement (NRI), with additional joint‐effect, interaction, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses.

Results

At the 2015 follow‐up, 406 participants (6.2%) met the criteria for CLMM. Higher CHG was linearly associated with incident CLMM (highest vs lowest quartile: OR 1.64, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.22–2.22). Obesity‐derived variants showed stronger associations, particularly CHG‐BMI (OR 3.13, 95% CI 2.21–4.43), which also had the highest discrimination (AUC 0.643, 95% CI 0.616–0.669) and continuous NRI (34.08%). Participants with both high CHG and high BMI had the greatest CLMM risk (OR 2.59, 95% CI 1.94–3.51). Results were robust in sensitivity analyses.

Conclusions

The CHG index and its obesity‐derived variants, particularly CHG‐BMI, are accessible and inexpensive candidate metabolic markers for predicting new‐onset CLMM. CHG‐BMI showed the highest apparent discrimination among the evaluated indices, although its AUC was modest. External population validation is needed.

Research Question

Are baseline CHG and its obesity- or inflammation-derived indices associated with new-onset cardiovascular–liver–metabolic multimorbidity, and how well do they discriminate follow-up cases within CHARLS?

Author Evidence Summary

This observational study related baseline metabolic indices to a later binary multimorbidity outcome in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Multimorbidity required at least two component conditions and included an LAP-based suspected MASLD phenotype rather than imaging-confirmed liver disease. CHG-BMI had a stronger quartile association and a higher apparent AUC than the other evaluated indices. The findings support candidate-marker research; they do not establish causation, independent validation, an actionable clinical threshold, or benefit from modifying CHG-BMI.

What This Study Adds

  • Compares CHG with obesity- and inflammation-derived variants for a prospectively ascertained, surrogate-defined multimorbidity endpoint.
  • Separates the strength of adjusted associations from the modest, apparent discrimination of the evaluated indices.
  • Reports joint CHG/BMI associations while distinguishing them from evidence of statistical interaction.

Evidence Scope

Supports

  • Associations between baseline indices and meeting study-defined CLMM criteria at follow-up in this CHARLS analysis.
  • CHG-BMI ranking highest among evaluated indices for apparent ROC discrimination in this sample.
  • Hypothesis generation and independent validation of accessible metabolic-marker combinations.

Does Not Establish

  • A causal effect of CHG, obesity, or inflammation on multimorbidity.
  • Imaging- or biopsy-confirmed MASLD in every participant.
  • Hazard ratios, exact onset timing, or a time-to-event prediction model.
  • Independent internal or external validation, clinical net benefit, treatment efficacy, or a validated clinical cut-off.

Limitations

  • Residual confounding and observational design prevent causal inference.
  • Exact component-disease onset dates were unavailable; logistic ORs cannot be interpreted as hazards or incidence rates.
  • The MASLD component used an LAP-based suspected phenotype and may be misclassified; metabolic components overlap conceptually with the exposure indices.
  • The ROC analyses used the same analysis sample without internal or external validation, so apparent AUCs may be optimistic.
  • Single baseline exposure measurements, substantial complete-data selection, medication changes, correlated comparisons, and exploratory subgroup analyses limit interpretation.
  • Generalizability beyond Chinese adults aged at least 45 years requires independent evaluation.

Q&A

Who was included in the analysis?

The final analytic cohort comprised 6,534 CHARLS participants aged at least 45 years without baseline CLMM. Baseline measurements were from 2011 and outcome ascertainment from 2015.

Evidence: KF1

What counted as cardiovascular–liver–metabolic multimorbidity?

Participants had to meet at least two criteria for CVD, type 2 diabetes, and LAP-based suspected MASLD at follow-up. The liver component was a surrogate phenotype, not universal imaging or histological confirmation.

Evidence: KF1

How strong was the association with CHG?

In the fully adjusted logistic model, the highest versus lowest CHG quartile had an OR of 1.64 with a 95% CI of 1.22–2.22. This is an observational odds ratio for the follow-up outcome.

Evidence: KF2

Did CHG-BMI perform better than CHG alone?

CHG-BMI had a stronger highest-versus-lowest-quartile association and the largest observed AUC among the compared indices. Its AUC of 0.643 was still modest and was evaluated in the analysis sample.

Evidence: KF3, KF4

Was this an externally validated clinical prediction model?

No. The article explicitly describes apparent, in-sample ROC evaluation without internal or external validation. Independent population validation and assessment beyond BMI and conventional risk factors are still needed.

Evidence: KF4

Did high CHG and high BMI interact statistically?

The jointly high group had greater follow-up CLMM odds than the low–low group, but the multiplicative interaction was not statistically significant. Joint association therefore does not establish synergy.

Evidence: KF5

Can these findings guide a treatment intended to prevent CLMM?

The study did not test an intervention. Observational associations and apparent discrimination do not establish that changing an index prevents disease or improves clinical decisions.

Evidence: KF2, KF3, KF4
