## Evidence Snapshot

**Associations between magnesium depletion score and depression among individuals aged 20 to 60 years**

A cross-sectional NHANES analysis examined a medication, renal-function and alcohol-based magnesium depletion score in relation to PHQ-9-defined depressive symptoms. The adjusted associations describe the surveyed analytic sample; they do not validate a depression diagnostic tool or demonstrate benefit from magnesium supplementation.

| Evidence | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Unique total | n=18,247 |
| Pooled NHANES 2005–2018 analytic sample | n=18,247 |

## Research Question

Among NHANES 2005–2018 adults aged 20–60 years, how was magnesium depletion score associated with a PHQ-9 score of at least 10 after survey-weighted covariate adjustment?

## Author Evidence Summary

The study pooled public survey data and compared concurrent MDS and PHQ-9 classifications. The main regression table supports a modest adjusted per-point association and higher odds in the MDS 2 and at least 3 categories. MDS is a proxy score, the outcome is questionnaire-defined, and reverse causation and residual confounding remain possible.

## Study Design & Analytical Framework

### Study profile

- Study Design: Secondary pooled cross-sectional analysis of NHANES 2005–2018
- Evidence Type: Survey-weighted human prevalence-odds association
- Unique total: 18,247
- Population: Adults aged 20–60 years after exclusions for missing PHQ-9, MDS and specified covariates and for pregnancy
- Primary Endpoint: PHQ-9 score at least 10 at the survey assessment
- Secondary Endpoint: The same PHQ-9 outcome in score-category, component, spline and subgroup analyses; no separate prospective endpoint
- External validation: No
- Data modalities: PHQ-9 symptom questionnaire, medication-use and alcohol variables, estimated glomerular filtration rate, demographic and health covariates, two 24-hour dietary recalls

## What This Study Adds

- Quantifies the association of an operational magnesium depletion score with concurrent PHQ-9 classification in a pooled NHANES adult sample.
- Separates the per-point association from score-category and component estimates, with their uncertainty.
- Provides spline and subgroup analyses while retaining the limits of non-significant nonlinearity and interaction tests.

## Evidence Scope

### Supports

- Reported survey-weighted MDS–PHQ-9 prevalence-odds associations in the included adults aged 20–60 years.
- Definition of the proxy score, questionnaire outcome, analytic denominator and covariate-adjustment set in the main article.
- Descriptive assessment of score categories, components, spline nonlinearity and subgroup interactions within the same survey sample.

### Does Not Establish

- That magnesium depletion causes depression, or that lowering MDS or magnesium supplementation prevents or treats depression.
- A validated clinical diagnosis, depression screening rule, individual prognosis or treatment threshold derived from MDS.
- Direct measurement of serum or whole-body magnesium, or proof that magnesium mediates the observed medication associations.
- Proof of linearity from P=0.084, identical effects across subgroups, or statistical significance in every subgroup.
- Numerical results from Supplementary Tables 1–2, which were referenced by the paper but not obtained for this audit.

### Limitations

- The cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal direction; reverse causation and residual confounding remain possible (Discussion, p. 5).
- MDS combines medication use, kidney function and drinking behavior; it is not a biochemical magnesium measurement, and serum-magnesium comparison was unavailable (Methods 2.3; Discussion, p. 5).
- PHQ-9 at least 10 is the study's questionnaire-based outcome, not an independently adjudicated clinical diagnosis (Methods 2.2).
- Participants with specified missing data were excluded. The main text reports use of recommended NHANES weights but does not identify the exact weight variable, pooled-weight construction or a missing-data imputation procedure; no participant-level reanalysis is claimed (Fig. 1; Methods 2.1 and 2.5).
- High MDS values were uncommon: scores 3, 4 and 5 contained 325, 41 and 2 participants and were pooled. High-score and subgroup estimates are correspondingly imprecise (Methods 2.3; Tables 2 and 4).
- Supplementary Tables 1–2 were not available for this audit. Dietary-threshold specifications and alternative-category sensitivity estimates from those files are not presented as verified results.
- Figure 2's caption uses beneficial-threshold language, but the design and results do not establish a beneficial or clinical intervention threshold. The nonlinearity P value does not prove linearity.
- Generalization beyond the included US adult survey population requires independent evidence.

## Q&A

### What population and outcome were actually analyzed?

The analytic sample comprised 18,247 NHANES 2005–2018 adults aged 20–60 years; 1,753 had PHQ-9 scores of at least 10. These are unweighted counts. The endpoint was a questionnaire threshold for recent symptoms, not an independently adjudicated diagnosis or new-onset depression.

Evidence: KF1

### Was magnesium measured directly?

No direct serum-magnesium comparison was available in this study. MDS combined diuretic use, proton pump inhibitor use, renal-function categories and heavy drinking into a 0–5 proxy score; the reported association does not validate the score against a biochemical deficiency measure.

Evidence: KF2

### What was the fully adjusted per-point association?

Survey-weighted Model 3 reported OR 1.15 per MDS point, with 95% CI 1.04–1.26 and P=0.0100. Adjustment included demographics, BMI, smoking, diabetes and dietary energy, calcium and magnesium. This is a concurrent prevalence-odds association, not prospective risk or a causal effect.

Evidence: KF3

### Did all MDS categories show the same evidence?

No. Compared with MDS 0, Model 3 estimates were 1.03 (0.87–1.21), 1.41 (1.12–1.78) and 1.58 (1.06–2.35) for scores 1, 2 and at least 3. The score-1 interval crosses 1; the highest category included only 368 participants. These categories do not establish a clinical action threshold.

Evidence: KF4

### Does the PPI component result show that treatment causes depression?

No. The PPI-use estimate was OR 2.24 (95% CI 1.78–2.80) in Model 3, but medication indications, residual confounding and the cross-sectional design prevent a medication-causation or magnesium-mediation conclusion. It is not evidence to stop a prescribed PPI.

Evidence: KF5

### Does P=0.084 establish a linear dose response or a beneficial threshold?

No. The spline test did not detect nonlinearity at the paper's 0.05 level. That is not proof of linearity, and the caption's beneficial-threshold wording does not establish a diagnostic or treatment cutoff.

Evidence: KF6

### Were the associations significant and identical in every subgroup?

No. All reported interaction P values exceeded 0.05, but several stratum-specific confidence intervals crossed 1 and some were wide. A non-significant interaction does not demonstrate equivalence or significance in each subgroup.

Evidence: KF7

### Can these results justify magnesium supplementation for depression?

No. This was an observational cross-sectional score analysis without a supplementation intervention or prospective depression endpoint. It supports an association requiring further research, not prevention or treatment efficacy.

Evidence: KF1, KF2, KF3
