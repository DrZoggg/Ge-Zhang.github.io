Official Abstract

Musculoskeletal disorders such as osteoarthritis (OA), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and osteoporosis (OP) affect millions worldwide, often progressing silently until advanced stages. Accessible early risk stratification remains challenging because conventional diagnosis often depends on imaging, specialist evaluation, and fragmented clinical information. Herein, we developed Luma AI, a multimodal AI platform that integrates routine blood tests and demographic variables with curated patient-reported symptom narratives. The text branch uses a domain-specific clinical text encoder based on LoRA-adapter-fine-tuned Bio_ClinicalBERT, whereas the structured branch uses optimized classical machine learning models. The two branches are fused through an explicit prediction-score and text-representation pipeline for early screening of OA, RA, and OP. Performance was validated using the U.S. NHANES and multi-center Chinese cohorts. We evaluated more than 50 predictive models and applied Bayesian optimization to refine the structured models, achieving over 80% accuracy with structured data alone and exceeding 90% in the multimodal validation cohort when symptom narratives were included. The platform incorporates explainable AI tools to identify key risk factors and provides personalized post-screening recommendations for lifestyle, diet, and medical follow-up. This study demonstrates that Luma AI can support scalable low-cost early risk screening and personalized management of OA, RA, and OP while remaining positioned as a risk-stratification aid rather than a stand-alone diagnostic replacement.

Research Question

How can routine blood-test/demographic predictions and patient symptom-text representations be integrated for musculoskeletal screening support, and what model and workflow evidence does the study report?

Author Evidence Summary

The study reports 58,777 NHANES records, with 49,523 from 1999–2016 for training and 9,254 from 2017–2018 for temporal testing. The Chinese structured evaluation used data from 4,436 patients across four centers. After benchmarking structured classifiers, the authors selected Extra Trees and used SHAP to examine which inputs drove predictions. Reported OA, RA and OP AUCs were 0.914, 0.841 and 0.954 in the temporal test set and 0.892, 0.805 and 0.915 in the Chinese cohorts. The article reports using both test sets to select hyperparameters and separately describes refitting before evaluation in its fusion protocol. Three Extra Trees scores and a 768-dimensional Bio_ClinicalBERT representation feed a 771-dimensional linear classifier. The architecture uses out-of-fold structured scores and curated, redacted symptom text. In a separate comparison using hospitalization records from 126 patients, the authors report 9.176% higher accuracy than blood-data-only models. The underlying counts and gain calculation are referred to Table S11. The platform also provides patient screening outputs, physician model training and SHAP, and post-screening information.

What This Study Adds

  • Provides a concrete fusion example: three disease-specific structured probabilities plus a clinical text-encoder representation, with explicitly described orchestration and training safeguards.
  • Provides structured-model estimates from temporal and multicenter settings and reports a separate hospital comparison with symptom text.
  • Connects model outputs with SHAP and patient/physician workflow tools, creating an applied context for studying interpretable, accessible-input screening support.

Evidence Scope

Supports

  • The reported data roles, structured-model comparison estimates and their specific source discrepancies.
  • The main-text two-stage fusion architecture, encoder/orchestration roles and described input safeguards.
  • SHAP/ablation descriptions and published platform/recommendation workflow components.

Does Not Establish

  • Corrected training intervals or independent evaluation of the complete frozen fusion pipeline.
  • Per-task denominators and the exact calculation of the 9.176% gain.
  • Prospective patient benefit, cost-effectiveness or dietary-intervention effects.

Limitations

  • Results 2.2 reports 42 candidate inputs while Figure 2 describes 41. All three printed training AUC points lie below their paired confidence intervals.
  • Both structured test sets were used in the configuration selection described in Results 2.2. Results 2.4 describes the refit and one-time application separately. The article does not resolve the stage-specific allocation needed to identify an untouched evaluation of the full fused pipeline.
  • Supporting Information S1 was unavailable for this summary. Tables S1–S2 are cited for cohort and predictor detail, S11 for the hospital accuracy comparison, S12–S13 for dietary synthesis, S15 for ablation and S20–S21 for complete fusion and code mapping.
  • SHAP and ablation characterize model reliance. Prospective patient benefit, cost-effectiveness and effects of dietary interventions require separate evaluation.

Q&A

Which datasets and counts does the study report?

NHANES supplied 58,777 records, with 49,523 from 1999–2016 for training and 9,254 from 2017–2018 for Test Set 1. Test Set 2 used data from 4,436 patients across four Chinese clinical centers. The fusion comparison separately used hospital records from 126 patients at Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital.

Evidence: KF1

How was the structured model selected?

The Results reports 42 candidate predictors, 25 selected with TabNet, and 50 classifiers benchmarked. Extra Trees was selected. Figure 2 describes 41 inputs, and all three training AUCs printed in Results lie outside their paired confidence intervals. The exact printed pairs are listed in the structured-model finding.

Evidence: KF2

What AUCs does the paper report for the two structured test sets?

For OA, RA and OP, respectively, the AUCs were 0.914, 0.841 and 0.954 in Test Set 1 and 0.892, 0.805 and 0.915 in Test Set 2. Results 2.2 says both sets were used to select configurations. Results 2.4 describes one-time application after structured-model refitting as part of the fusion protocol.

Evidence: KF3

Which inputs stood out in SHAP and ablation analyses?

For OA, prominent features included age, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration and total protein. For RA, they included albumin, globulin and osmolality. For OP, they included age, sex and red-cell measures. The paper also reports an AUC decrease from 0.848 to 0.720 after removing age and sex, with the detailed task mapping referred to Table S15.

Evidence: KF4

How are structured outputs and symptom text fused?

The head receives three Extra Trees probabilities and a 768-dimensional Bio_ClinicalBERT [CLS] representation. It learns to classify OA, RA, OP and an other category from 771 features. The paper describes inner fivefold out-of-fold scores for training, LoRA tuning with redacted symptom text and fixed branches during fusion-head training. LangChain orchestrates inference.

Evidence: KF5

What accuracy gain did the authors report for symptom-text fusion?

In hospital records from 126 patients, the authors report 9.176% higher accuracy than blood-data-only models. The main text refers to Table S11 for the comparison and gives no exact class counts or split, or definition of relative gain versus percentage points.

Evidence: KF6

What post-screening and physician functions are described?

The patient portal returns disease probabilities and provides follow-up information. The physician portal offers model training, comparison across ten configurations, inference and SHAP views. The paper attributes dietary guidance to review and meta-analytic structural equation modeling of 25 indicators, with details cited in Tables S12–S13.

Evidence: KF7

What materials can researchers reuse and what is the intended clinical role?

NHANES is public. Hospital data are subject to the access and ethics conditions in the article, and the authors state that code will be shared upon request. The article also provides a link to a grid and randomized search tuning app. The intended clinical role is screening and decision support.

Evidence: KF1, KF5, KF7
