Ge Zhang 张格

张格（Ge Zhang），临床医学与计算生物医学研究者，主要从事 AI for Science、系统医学与转化生物医学研究。其研究面向重大慢性疾病，融合可解释人工智能、多模态临床数据与多组学研究体系，旨在解析疾病异质性、识别关键机制驱动因素，并推动分子特征向具有临床意义的生物学认知与转化标志物延伸。 心血管疾病，尤其是动脉粥样硬化，是其重要疾病研究场景，重点关注动脉粥样硬化血管微环境、血管平滑肌细胞可塑性及昼夜节律紊乱。 其研究贯穿人群尺度临床建模、多模态与多组学整合、计算推断、机制解析与转化验证，致力于建立从分子与细胞状态到临床表型及疾病结局的跨尺度关联。

Ge Zhang is a clinician-scientist working at the intersection of AI for Science, systems medicine, and translational biomedicine. His research integrates explainable artificial intelligence, multimodal clinical data, and multi-omics profiling to resolve disease heterogeneity, uncover mechanistic determinants, and translate molecular signatures into clinically actionable knowledge across major chronic diseases. Cardiovascular disease, particularly atherosclerosis, represents a major disease context in his research, with specific interests in the vascular microenvironment, vascular smooth muscle cell plasticity, and circadian disruption. His work spans population-scale clinical modeling, multimodal and multi-omics integration, computational inference, mechanistic interrogation, and translational validation, establishing cross-scale links from molecular and cellular states to clinical phenotypes and disease outcomes.

Research themes

Four connected directions

AI for Science & Multimodal Clinical Intelligence

Explainable AI, multimodal clinical modeling, survival and longitudinal analysis, and decision-support frameworks for risk stratification and translational discovery across major chronic diseases.

Multi-omics & Translational Discovery

Integration of transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and single-cell data to resolve disease heterogeneity, prioritize mechanistic drivers, and advance biomarker translation.

Atherosclerosis & Vascular Systems Biology

Plaque vulnerability, vascular microenvironment, vascular smooth muscle cell plasticity, single-cell states, and cross-scale mechanisms of vascular remodeling.

Circadian Disruption & Disease Biology

Systems-level investigation of circadian disruption, chronobiology, and vascular inflammation, linking temporal dysregulation to molecular states, disease progression, and clinical phenotypes.
